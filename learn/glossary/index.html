A

Action potential
The electrical event that passes along a heart cell and triggers it to contract. In heart muscle it lasts around 300 ms, roughly a hundred times longer than a nerve’s.
Afterload
The load the ventricle has to push against to eject blood. Raising it makes the heart work harder for less output.
Angina
Chest discomfort caused by heart muscle short of oxygen. Often pressure rather than pain, and frequently felt in the arm, neck or jaw. It is the LAST stage of the ischemic cascade, not the first.
Arterial compliance
How much volume the arterial tree stores for each mmHg of pressure. It is what turns a pulsed ejection into nearly steady flow downstream, and it falls both with age and with rising pressure inside a single beat.
Asystole
No electrical activity in the heart at all, a flat line. Not shockable, because a defibrillator resets a rhythm and cannot create one.
Autoregulation
The ability of small vessels to dilate or constrict to keep blood flow steady despite changes in pressure. It is what preserves resting flow past a narrowing, and what a stenosis consumes first.

C

Cardiac output
How much blood the heart moves per minute: heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. About 5 L/min at rest.
Censoring
In a study, a participant known to be event-free up to some point and unknown after it, because they moved away or the study ended. Kaplan–Meier exists to use their partial information without over-claiming.
Conductance
How easily blood passes through a vascular bed, and the reciprocal of resistance. Beds sit in parallel, so an organ receives whatever share of cardiac output its conductance represents.
Confidence interval
A range of values compatible with the data. A wide one means the study could not narrow the answer down, which is not the same as the answer being zero.
Coronary flow reserve
How much more flow the heart could get if it needed it: maximal flow divided by resting flow. Normally 3–5. A narrowing consumes this long before it reduces resting flow.
Coronary perfusion pressure
The pressure difference driving blood through the heart’s own arteries: diastolic pressure minus the pressure inside the ventricle. During CPR it is the strongest predictor of getting a circulation back.
CPR
Chest compressions. They generate roughly a fifth of a normal cardiac output. They do not restart the heart; they buy time and keep the rhythm shockable.

D

Defibrillation
A shock that depolarizes the whole heart at once so it can reorganize. It works only when there is a disorganized rhythm to reset.
Depolarization
A cell’s interior becoming less negative, usually because positive ions rush in. In the heart it is what triggers contraction.
Diastole
The relaxed part of the cardiac cycle, when the ventricles fill, and when the heart muscle feeds itself.
Diastolic dysfunction
The ventricle failing to relax properly, so it stiffens and filling pressure rises. It appears BEFORE contraction weakens, because relaxation is the step that costs energy.

E

Ejection fraction
The proportion of blood in the ventricle that is ejected each beat, normally around 55–70 %. Not the same as cardiac output.
End-systolic elastance
The slope of the line linking pressure and volume at the end of every squeeze. It is the measure of contractility that stays put when filling or afterload changes, which no single pressure–volume loop can give you.

F

Filling pressure (LVEDP)
The pressure in the left ventricle at the end of filling. It rises when the ventricle stiffens, which is an early sign of trouble.

G

Glagov remodeling
An artery expanding outward as plaque accumulates, preserving the channel until plaque occupies around 40 % of the cross-section. The reason coronary disease is silent for decades.

H

Hazard
An instantaneous rate of events, measured per person-year. Integrating it over time gives a probability.

I

Ischemia
Tissue receiving less blood than it needs. In the heart it begins in the inner layer of the wall and spreads outward as it worsens.
Isovolumetric
A phase of the beat with every valve shut, so pressure changes while volume cannot. One happens on the way up and one on the way down, and they are the vertical sides of the pressure–volume loop.

K

Kaplan–Meier curve
An estimate of how long a group goes before an event, built so that participants who leave the study early still contribute the time they were observed. It steps down only at observed events.

M

Mean arterial pressure
The average pressure across a whole cardiac cycle. It sits closer to the diastolic number than the systolic one because diastole lasts longer, and it is what perfusion actually depends on.
MET
A unit of effort. One MET is quiet sitting; every extra MET is another 3.5 mL of oxygen per kilogram per minute the circulation has to deliver. A brisk walk is about 4, running about 10.

O

Oxygen delivery
Flow multiplied by the oxygen content of the blood: how much oxygen arrives per minute. Anemia and a narrowed artery reduce it by different routes, and the tissue downstream cannot tell them apart.
Oxygen extraction ratio
The fraction of delivered oxygen a tissue actually takes up: around 25 % for the body as a whole at rest, and near 70 % for heart muscle even at rest. A tissue with little extraction left has no reserve when supply falls.

P

p-value
The probability of seeing data at least this extreme if there were no real effect. NOT the probability that a treatment works, and a large one usually means the study was too small.
Peripheral resistance
The combined resistance of every vascular bed downstream of the aorta. Together with cardiac output it sets mean arterial pressure, and it is the term a vasodilator moves.
Plateau (phase 2)
The long flat part of the cardiac action potential, carried by calcium entering the cell. It is unique to heart muscle and is why the heart cannot be tetanised.
Preload
How full the ventricle is before it contracts. More filling means a bigger squeeze, up to a point. This is the Frank–Starling relationship.
Pulse pressure
Systolic minus diastolic. It reflects the stroke volume delivered and the stiffness of the aorta receiving it, so it widens with age even when the average pressure has not moved.

R

Refractory period
The window after a beat during which a cell cannot be re-excited. In heart muscle it lasts nearly as long as the contraction, which is what makes sustained cramp impossible.

S

Silent ischemia
Heart muscle measurably short of oxygen while the person feels nothing. Common, and commoner in diabetes where the sensory nerves are damaged.
Splanchnic circulation
The circulation of the gut, liver and spleen, taking roughly a quarter of resting cardiac output. It is the largest reservoir the body draws on during exercise or bleeding.
Stenosis
A narrowing. Usually quoted as a percentage of the vessel’s diameter, and a 50 % diameter narrowing removes 75 % of the cross-sectional area.
Stroke volume
How much blood leaves the ventricle in one beat. About 70 mL at rest; well over 100 in a trained heart under load.
Stroke work
The mechanical work one beat performs, measured as the area inside the pressure–volume loop. Work done against pressure costs more oxygen than the same work done by moving more volume.
Systole
The contracting part of the cardiac cycle, when blood is ejected, and when the heart’s own supply is squeezed shut.

V

Ventricular fibrillation
The ventricles quivering rather than contracting, with every cell firing on its own schedule. No output at all. Shockable, and the most survivable cause of cardiac arrest.
Vulnerable plaque
A plaque likely to rupture: a thin fibrous cap over a large soft lipid core. Often not the plaque causing the most narrowing, and that is why most heart attacks arise from lesions that were not flow-limiting beforehand.
